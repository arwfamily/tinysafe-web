Shapes to Avoid

Recalls are issued to brands. But when dozens of different brands making the same shape are all recalled for the same hazard, the brand was never the problem. The shape was.

How we measured. We took every recall in our corpus — 6,266 US notices back to 1972 and 33,949 Canadian notices back to 1991 — grouped them by product structure rather than brand, and computed one number for each: hazard concentration, the share of that structure's recalls naming a single hazard. A structure with 90% concentration across decades and dozens of unrelated companies is not suffering from bad manufacturing. It is a design that does not work around children.

01 — The proof that this method works

Drop-side cribs

37US notices
92%name entrapment
26Canadian notices
0since 2013

A crib with a side rail that slides down. Thirty-seven separate companies were recalled, and 92% of those notices describe the same failure: the rail detaches at one corner, opening a V-shaped gap between rail and mattress, and an infant slides into it head-down. Canada recalled the same structure 26 times.

This entry is here because it is the closing argument for the whole page. Regulators spent 24 years recalling brand after brand. In 2011 the US banned the structure — every drop-side crib, regardless of maker. New recalls dropped to zero and have stayed there. Nothing about manufacturing quality changed. The shape was removed, and the deaths stopped.

Notices: CPSC 10-134 (3 deaths) · CPSC 13-151 (last of its kind, 2013).

Instead: any fixed-side crib made to the current standard. Never buy a drop-side crib secondhand, and never accept one as a hand-me-down — they are banned from resale in the US, but they still circulate in marketplace listings and family basements.

02 — The deadliest structure in the record

Inclined sleepers, rockers, and sleep-capable swings

16US notices
88%name suffocation
100deaths, one notice
2025still recurring

Any product that holds a baby to sleep at an angle. The mechanism is simple and unforgiving: an infant's head falls forward onto the chest, or the baby rolls to the side against a soft surface, and the airway closes. A newborn cannot lift their own head out of it.

One notice alone — the Rock 'n Play re-announcement, CPSC 23-088 — reports 100 deaths. That is the largest death figure attached to any single structure in our corpus.

Watch the shape migrate. After inclined sleepers were regulated, the same angle reappeared inside a different product category: infant swings advertised or usable for sleep. In November 2025 the CPSC warned about CPSC 26-082 and CPSC 26-074, both for creating an unsafe sleeping environment. The category name changed. The angle did not.

Instead: a flat, firm, bare surface for every sleep — crib, bassinet, or play yard meeting the current standard. If a product reclines and mentions sleep, treat those two facts together as the warning.

03 — The one still flooding the market

Pillow-like infant loungers and nursing pillows used for sleep

43US notices
66%name suffocation
12in 2026 alone
8deaths, one notice

Soft, padded, walled nests marketed for supervised lounging — and used, inevitably, for sleep. The recall language is nearly identical every time: the sides are too soft, the product fails the mandatory standards for infant sleep products or infant support cushions, and an infant's airway can be obstructed. CPSC 21-198 (Boppy loungers) reports 8 deaths.

This is the structure to understand if you want to see how the market actually behaves. In 2026 alone we count twelve separate lounger and nursing-pillow notices, from twelve brand names most parents have never heard of, all citing the same standard. Recalling them one at a time is not working, because the brands are disposable and the shape is not.

Instead: use a nursing pillow only for feeding, while you are holding the baby, and put it away afterward. Nothing padded and walled belongs where a baby might fall asleep.

04 — The one that isn't sold as a baby product

Corded window blinds and shades

55US notices
95%name strangulation
106Canadian notices
48years of the same hazard

Forty-eight years, 55 US notices, and 95% of them say the same word: strangulation. A pull cord, an inner lift cord, or a looped chain forms a loop at a toddler's height; a child in a crib or playpen reaches it. Canada issued 106 notices for the same structure, strangulation dominant there too. Two regulators, working separately, reached one conclusion.

We include this because it is the most under-defended hazard in a nursery. Blinds are not sold as baby products, appear on no registry, and are usually already installed in the room before the crib arrives — so no one ever "decides" to buy them. Notices: CPSC 10-073 (5 deaths) · CPSC 25-479 (2025, 133,000 units).

Instead: cordless blinds or exterior-mounted shades in any room a child uses. Cordless is now the industry default — the fix is cheap, permanent, and available at any hardware store.

05 — Small hazard count, catastrophic mechanism

High-powered magnet sets, balls, and building sticks

126US notices
86%name ingestion
113Canadian notices
24in 2026 alone

Loose neodymium magnets small enough to swallow. Two swallowed at different times find each other through the walls of the intestine and clamp — pinching off blood supply, perforating the bowel. There is no external injury, often no immediate symptom, and surgery is frequently required. The most recent notices state that the agency is aware of 8 deaths and estimates roughly 2,400 magnet ingestions treated in US emergency departments each year.

Twenty-four notices in 2026 alone, most of them for products sold under interchangeable marketplace brand names: CPSC 26-686, CPSC 26-698, CPSC 26-664.

Instead: magnetic tiles and blocks where the magnets are sealed inside large plastic pieces a child cannot swallow. Keep loose high-powered magnet sets — including adult desk toys and magnetic jewelry — out of any home with children under 14.

06 — Highest concentration on this page

Unanchored dressers and chests of drawers

91US notices
97%name tip-over
24in 2026 alone
30years running

Ninety-seven percent — the highest concentration we measured. A child opens the drawers to climb, the drawers act as a lever, the centre of mass leaves the footprint, and the chest falls onto them. Tall, narrow, lightweight chests with full-extension drawers are the worst geometry.

What makes this structure different from the others here is that it is fixable rather than avoidable: you will own a dresser. Notices: CPSC 16-204 (4 deaths) · CPSC 26-690 and CPSC 26-663 (both August 2026).

Instead: buy furniture that passes the current stability standard, and anchor everything taller than it is wide to a wall stud — including the one already in the room. Anchoring hardware costs a few dollars and is the single highest-value safety purchase in a nursery.

07 — Banned, and still being sold

Crib bumper pads

24US notices
83%name suffocation
7in 2026 alone
36years of the same finding

Padded liners tied around the inside of a crib. An infant who rolls face-first into one cannot reliably turn away. The Safe Sleep for Babies Act banned padded crib bumpers in the US in 2022 — and yet seven notices were issued in 2026 alone, all for bumpers still reaching buyers through online marketplaces: CPSC 26-587, CPSC 26-534, CPSC 26-495.

The lesson worth carrying: a ban is not the same as absence. Banned structures keep arriving through third-party sellers, and the buyer is the last line of defence.

Instead: nothing. A bare crib is the finished product. If you are worried about limbs slipping through slats, the current slat spacing standard already prevents it; mesh liners marketed as "breathable" still add material to a sleep surface, and the safest crib has none.

08 — The structure that creates false confidence

Baby bath seats and bath rings

23US notices
87%name tip-over
83%name drowning
4in 2026 alone

Suction-cup seats and rings that hold a baby upright in a tub. Two hazard words appear together in almost every notice, and they are one chain: the seat is unstable and tips, or the leg openings let the child slip through, and a baby who cannot right themselves is face-down in water. Notices: CPSC 26-441, CPSC 26-288, CPSC 26-108 — all 2026, all citing instability against the mandatory bath seat standard.

The deeper problem is psychological rather than mechanical. A product that holds the baby invites the caregiver to let go — to reach for a towel, to answer the door. Drowning takes very little water and very little time.

Instead: a few inches of water in a plain infant tub or the sink, with one hand on the baby the whole time. No device replaces the hand.

09 — Hidden inside things that look like toys

Products with accessible button-cell batteries

23US notices
100%name ingestion
11in 2026 alone
2016first notice

Every single notice in this group names the same hazard — the only 100% on this page. A swallowed coin cell lodges in the oesophagus and begins an electrical reaction that burns through tissue within hours. The battery does not need to be leaking or old.

What makes this structural rather than incidental is where the batteries hide: light-up party favours, novelty keychains, singing greeting cards, small remotes, flameless candles, light-up shoes. CPSC 26-580 (light-up party favours, 13,400 units) and CPSC 26-650 are recent examples. Reese's Law now requires secured compartments and warnings on consumer products — which is exactly why non-compliant imports keep getting recalled.

Instead: require a screw-secured battery compartment on anything a child can reach. Treat cheap light-up novelties as ineligible for a home with a crawler, and know that the emergency response to a suspected ingestion is immediate — this is an emergency-room visit, not a wait-and-see.

10 — Happening right now

Long-cord pull-string teething toys

8US notices
88%name choking
2026all within one year
3countries

Soft toys with long, thin silicone strings a baby pulls and chews. The strings are narrower and longer than the mandatory toy standard permits: they can reach the back of a child's throat and lodge there, and on some models the small silicone piece on the end detaches. Eight US notices in a single year — CPSC 26-671, CPSC 26-635, CPSC 26-562 among them — plus notices in the United Kingdom and Australia.

We include a structure this new to make a point about timing. All eight recalls happened after these toys had already been sold and used. A parent who understood the shape — long thin cord, on a toy designed for the mouth — could have declined all eight before any notice existed.

Instead: a teether that is one solid piece, larger than the child's mouth, with no strings, cords, or detachable ends.

What this page is not

Honesty is the reason this page is worth citing, so here are its limits.

Not every recalled structure is on this list. We required a high concentration of a single hazard across many notices and many years. Baby walkers, for example, have 40 US notices but no dominant hazard — the 1997 standard changed the design, and the record reflects a structure that was fixed rather than one that cannot be fixed. That is a real distinction, and this page is about the second kind.

Counts are notices, not products. One notice can cover a hundred models; a re-announcement of an earlier recall is counted again. Death figures are the highest number stated in a single official notice — never a sum, because agencies restate cumulative national totals in every notice of a category, and adding those up would multiply the same deaths many times over. Where we say "8 deaths" for magnets, that is the agency's cumulative national figure as of the most recent notice, not eight deaths caused by one product.

A structure being absent from this list is not an endorsement. And the absence of a recall never means a product is safe. It means no notice has been issued yet.

How to use this in a store or a registry. Ask three questions of the shape in front of you, not the logo on it. Does it hold a baby at an angle for sleep? Does it put anything soft, padded, or looped inside a sleep space? Does it place a cord, a small magnet, or a coin battery within a child's reach? Those three questions cover nine of the ten structures above — and they work on products that have not been invented yet.
