Chapter 1 — The Numbers

How much of the world's baby-product safety record can actually be read?

TinySafe maintains a continuously updated corpus of official product-safety records across six jurisdictions, normalized into a single schema. This report is computed directly from that corpus. Every number in it can be traced to an official government notice.

What makes this corpus different from checking any single government website:

  • It is normalized. A recall's hazard, remedy, and record type read the same way whether the notice came from Washington, Ottawa, or Brussels — so patterns can be counted, not just browsed.
  • It preserves severity as officially stated. US death and injury counts, FDA Class I–III, EU risk levels, and Canadian hazard classes are kept in their original form.
  • It includes what was never fixed. Alongside recalls, the corpus holds safety warnings — hazards announced with no remedy offered. The US record alone contains 211 warnings; in the EU record, market withdrawals without consumer remedy are the majority of actions.
  • It links across borders. 322 Canadian notices explicitly declare a joint recall with a US counterpart. The corpus links them, then adds registry-level and barcode-level matches on top.
A note on honesty. Coverage is not symmetrical. The EU backfill is still in progress; some sources date notices only to the week or the last update; and death counts always mean "reported in official recall notices," never our own tally. Wherever a number below carries a limitation, the limitation is stated next to the number.

Chapter 2 — What Every Country Agrees On

Three categories keep appearing in every country's record

When the same product category is recalled, for the same class of hazard, in the United States, Canada, and the European Union — under three regulatory systems that do not coordinate their enforcement — that is not noise. That is the closest thing product safety has to a global consensus.

We scanned all three bodies with identical category definitions. "Severe" is defined per jurisdiction: US = highest alert tier or a death reported in the notice; Canada and EU = strangulation, choking, suffocation, entrapment, or drowning named in the notice.

1. Children's clothing — the category nobody suspects

The largest common category is also the least suspected one. And the three systems see different deaths in the same garment: North American notices overwhelmingly cite flammability standards — children's sleepwear that ignites — while EU notices overwhelmingly cite strangulation by hood and waist cords. 299 of the EU's 412 clothing notices are severe, the highest severity share of any category in any jurisdiction. Example notices: CPSC 24-301 (children's pajama sets, joint with Canada), Health Canada 82369 (children's pajamas, flammability, 2026).

2. Toys — the largest volume, the highest death count

Across the three bodies, more than 2,000 toy notices cite choking on small parts. US notices in this category report 119 deaths. Within toys, high-powered magnets deserve their own line: fewer notices, far deadlier — 80 deaths reported in US notices, and 16 of the EU record's 18 magnet notices classified severe. When two swallowed magnets find each other through intestinal walls, the injury is internal and silent. This is why every jurisdiction that sees them treats them as an emergency.

3. Window blind cords — the danger that isn't sold as a baby product

The finding we most want parents to keep: corded window blinds appear in all three records at severity levels rivaling any nursery product — 392 EU notices, and 46 of Canada's 49 classified severe, with 16 deaths in the US record. Blinds are never sold as baby products, appear on no registry checklist, and are usually installed in the room before the crib arrives.

What is not universal

Honesty requires the reverse finding too. Infant sleep products — loungers and inclined sleepers — are a North American phenomenon in the record. US notices report 104 deaths; the EU record, in its current slice, shows almost none. Whether that reflects market differences, regulatory scope, or the still-incomplete EU backfill is a question we will revisit when the backfill completes — and we would rather say that plainly than overstate a global pattern.
